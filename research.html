Chemistry that never sits still

Most of the theory and software chemists use was built for molecules at rest, at or relaxing towards equilibrium. Life, and the molecular machines chemists are learning to build, run on a constant flow of energy. We develop the theory to understand and design them.

From a mug of hot water to molecular machines

Put a small Stirling engine on a mug of hot water on a snowy day and it starts to spin. Heat flows from hot to cold, and the engine turns part of that flow into motion. Thermodynamics tells us how such engines work; engineering tells us how to make them better.

Shrink the engine to the size of a molecule and the rules change. Molecules are constantly kicked around by thermal noise, so a molecular machine cannot simply push: it has to ratchet, using energy to bias random motion in one direction. Biology is full of such ratchets. They contract our muscles, copy DNA and make ATP.

Chemists have started to build artificial ratchets, but designing them is still largely serendipitous. Closing that gap is what we do.

Try it: a flashing ratchet

This is the simplest machine that turns random motion into directed motion. It runs a real simulation of Brownian particles, the same kind of physics we use for molecular motors.

Each dot is a particle jiggling in a lopsided, sawtooth-shaped energy landscape, closed by a wall at each end; the white one is a single particle you can follow. The plot underneath shows where all the particles are.

Energy landscape

Landscape switching on and off: the particles march right and pile up against the right wall.

Only switching gives directed motion: the lopsided teeth rectify the random jiggling, and the energy spent switching pays for it. Piling particles up against a wall is what a molecular pump does when it builds a concentration gradient; real pumps and motors run on chemical fuel or light. Changing the landscape keeps the particles where they are; “Start again” puts them back.

Functional reaction networks

How does a network’s structure decide what it can do?

Living cells depend on chemistry that only works away from equilibrium: pumps that build concentration gradients, proofreading steps that select the right partner, clocks that keep time. Each can be described as a network of reactions fed by a chemical fuel. Chemists now build artificial versions of some of these functions, but there are no general rules for which network delivers which function.

We are after those rules. Using chemical reaction network theory, we relate a network’s structure (which steps are coupled, which are driven, how asymmetric the kinetics are) to what it can do and how much free energy it stores and dissipates. With experimental partners, Emanuele has applied these ideas to active transport across liquid membranes and in large unilamellar vesicles, to information ratchets that make molecular recognition more selective, and to catalysis. Next, we want rules general enough to say which network to build for a given function.

Molecular machines by design

Can we design a molecular motor before anyone makes it?

ATP synthase, the motor that makes most of the ATP in your cells, runs in both directions: it uses a proton gradient to make fuel, or burns fuel to pump protons. That reversibility is the hallmark of a machine that truly transduces energy. The artificial molecular motors made so far only run one way, consuming fuel to produce motion.

We build coarse-grained simulations in which molecules are made of interacting beads and fuel is supplied by chemical reservoirs. They let us watch thousands of motor cycles, screen design ideas in days before anyone commits to months of synthesis, and use optimisation and machine learning to search for motors that couple fuel and motion tightly. From single motors we scale up to many coupled motors and to soft materials that contract like muscles.

Programmable electrochemistry

What is the best way to drive chemistry with electricity?

Running reactions directly on electricity is one route to a cleaner chemical industry. A recent twist is to flip the polarity of the electrodes many times per second, which makes some previously awkward reactions selective and scalable. Yet the waveforms in use are simple and symmetric, while the redox steps they drive are anything but symmetric.

We treat the waveform as something to design. Thermodynamically consistent reaction–diffusion models predict how a reaction responds to any driving protocol and how much energy it wastes; optimisation then finds the protocol that gives the best yield or efficiency. And we test the models against experiments, including our own: Emanuele trained in experimental electrochemistry during his Marie Skłodowska-Curie fellowship in Strasbourg.

The common thread: follow the energy

Whatever the system, we ask where the energy comes from, where it goes and how much of it does useful work.

Papers that quantify stored free energy
  1. Energy inChemical fuel, light or electricity
  2. Driving processFuel is consumed, photons absorbed, electrons transferred
  3. Driven processMotion, transport, synthesis against equilibrium
  4. Useful outputWork, stored free energy, function

At every step some energy is dissipated as heat. Our theory quantifies how much is lost, and where.

With colleagues, Emanuele has helped develop a nonequilibrium thermodynamic theory of chemical reaction networks that answers these questions for chemistry driven by fuels, light and electricity. It made it possible to quantify the efficiency of synthetic molecular motors and pumps from experimental data, and it is the shared language of everything we do.

Key papers: Nat. Commun. 2019, Nat. Chem. 2022, Nat. Nanotechnol. 2022, Chem 2024.

Our toolbox

From pen and paper to the electrochemistry bench. You do not need to master all of it when you join; you will learn the parts your project needs.

Analytical theory

Chemical reaction networks, nonequilibrium thermodynamics and stochastic processes: the equations behind the design rules.

Molecular simulations

Coarse-grained Langevin dynamics with chemical reservoirs, in our own Julia code and in LAMMPS, on Mainz’s HPC clusters.

Multiphysics models

Reaction–diffusion equations, control theory and optimisation in Python and COMSOL, to predict and shape driven processes.

Electrochemistry bench

Programmable potentiostat experiments that test our models and feed them with data.
