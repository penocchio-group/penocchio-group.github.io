Research/Stored free energy

How far from equilibrium?

Fuels, light and electricity can hold chemical systems away from equilibrium, and a system held there stores free energy. This page collects papers that have put a number on it, and explains how to compute it from concentrations.

See the papers How to compute it

What it measures

Molecular pumps, ratchets, ratchet-driven syntheses and kinetically trapped products are built from very different chemistry. The free energy they store is one way to describe them in the same terms.

A system that is kept away from equilibrium holds free energy that it would lose if it were left alone. This stored free energy is a useful number for describing a nonequilibrium state. It measures how far the system is from equilibrium. At constant temperature and pressure it is the most non-expansion work (chemical or electrical, for example) that could be extracted as the system returns to its reference equilibrium, and the least work needed to create the state from that equilibrium; keeping a state steady, as under continuous irradiation, costs additional, continuous dissipation.

For reactions in dilute solution it depends only on the temperature and on the concentrations in the driven state and at equilibrium. These usually come from the same NMR, UV–vis or HPLC data used to follow the chemistry; when an equilibrium concentration is too small to measure, as for a high-energy photoisomer, it is obtained from an equilibrium constant or a computed free-energy difference.

Energetic quantities like this one complement the kinetic descriptions, such as ratcheting constants, that are more often used for molecular machines, as we argued in a tutorial review written with Giulio Ragazzon and co-workers (Sangchai et al. 2023). The review also points out that counting only the energy that a ratchet’s own components exchange with the fuel or the light allows “more meaningful comparisons between ratchet systems powered by different energy sources or operated in different conditions”.

The expression itself has a long history in physical chemistry and stochastic thermodynamics (see below). In the past few years several experimental groups have reported it for their own systems, some of them in collaboration with us. This page lists those papers and explains how to compute the quantity.

Computing it

You need three things: the concentrations in the driven state, the concentrations at the reference equilibrium, and the temperature.

Stored free energy per unit volume, ideal dilute solution G−Geq = RT ∑X ( [X] ln⁡ [X] [X]eq − [X] + [X]eq ) ≥0
  • [X]Concentration of species X in the driven state, in mol L⁻¹. The sum runs over the species of the driven system, for example the co-conformations of a rotaxane or the reactants and products of a driven reaction.
  • [X]eqIts concentration at the reference equilibrium: usually the state the same system relaxes to when the driving is switched off, with the same total amount of each building block (see “Which equilibrium?” below for other choices).
  • RTGas constant times temperature: 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹ at 25 °C.
  • G − GeqFree energy of the driven mixture minus that of the equilibrium mixture, per unit volume (J L⁻¹). It is zero at equilibrium and positive for any other state with the same total amount of each building block. At constant temperature and pressure it is the most work that could be extracted as the system relaxes, and the least work needed to bring it there from equilibrium; holding a state steady costs extra, continuous dissipation. It describes the whole mixture; a reaction Gibbs energy, ΔrG (kJ mol⁻¹), describes one reaction.
Before you compute · 1

Which equilibrium?

The number depends on which equilibrium you compare with. Papers in the list use thermal equilibrium in the dark, a “local” equilibrium in which the ratio of photoisomers is kept fixed, or the equilibrium reached once the fuel has been converted to waste. Each answers a slightly different question. When fuel and waste are held at fixed concentrations (chemostatted), they are left out of the sum, and the reference is the equilibrium the system would reach if fuel and waste were at equilibrium with each other; the quantity is then a transformed, or semigrand, free energy (Rao and Esposito 2016; Penocchio et al. 2019). In batch experiments, where the fuel is used up, the reference is the equilibrium reached once it has been converted to waste.

Before you compute · 2

Dilute solutions

The expression uses concentrations in place of activities. That is usually fine at millimolar concentrations, but with strong interactions, high concentrations or many charges an extra term from the interactions between species appears, and the ideal expression is only an approximation (Avanzini et al. 2021).

Before you compute · 3

Per litre or per mole

The stored free energy is an amount per litre, so it grows with concentration. Dividing by the concentration of the machine or of the driven reactants gives kJ mol⁻¹. For unimolecular processes, such as shuttling or isomerisation, this removes the concentration dependence; for bimolecular ones, such as binding or synthesis, the equilibrium itself depends on concentration and the choice of normalising concentration is partly arbitrary. In compartmentalised systems the value also depends on the volume it refers to. Dividing by the free energy supplied by the fuel or the light gives an efficiency. For these reasons the list gives the conditions with every value.

    Where the expression comes from

    The quantity is not new. What is new is that experimental chemists now report it for their own systems.

    The idea that a system away from equilibrium holds energy that could be turned into work goes back to the founders of thermodynamics. For a mixture of reacting species, the free energy measured from equilibrium takes the form of a relative entropy between the actual and the equilibrium concentrations. Shear (1967) proposed it as a Lyapunov function for mass-action reaction networks, a chemical analogue of Boltzmann’s H-theorem. It is one for closed networks that satisfy detailed balance, but not in general for open ones (Higgins 1968), and Horn and Jackson (1972) proved that it is one for the wider class of complex-balanced networks. Hill (1977) is a standard reference for free-energy transduction and efficiency in biochemical cycles, and Qian (2001) identified relative entropy with the free energy of nonequilibrium deviations.

    That the excess free energy of a nonequilibrium distribution bounds the work it can deliver was recognised in statistical mechanics (Procaccia and Levine 1976). Stochastic thermodynamics, formulated for chemical reaction networks by Schmiedl and Seifert (2007), made it precise as the least work needed to create a nonequilibrium state and the most work it can deliver (Esposito and Van den Broeck 2011), and Sivak and Crooks (2012) showed how it can be measured. Rao and Esposito (2016) carried this over to open chemical reaction networks described by concentrations.

    Our own papers apply the framework to fuel- and light-driven reactions (2019, 2021) and to non-ideal solutions (2021), and a tutorial review written with Giulio Ragazzon and co-workers (2023) works through examples from experiments.
